About me

I'm Ajara Oyinloye Lawal, a data scientist with a background in software development. I work on healthcare AI, medical imaging and computer vision, and I'm most interested in how reliable machine learning models really are: how they behave under difficult conditions, where they fail, and how careful evaluation can bring those limits to light.

Medical imaging and model robustness

In a team research project for Carnegie Mellon University's Introduction to Deep Learning course, we're testing how robust skin-lesion classifiers for Mpox are. My part has been reproducing a published DenseNet121 baseline and training model variants to compare against it, including adversarially trained ones. We're looking at how adversarial perturbations affect classification, and at the trade-off between accuracy on clean images and resistance to attack. The work is shaping how I think about rigorous evaluation in medical image analysis.

Cardiovascular AI research

I volunteer with Carnegie Mellon University Africa's AI Healthcare Research Laboratory on CardioSync, a research initiative developing a multimodal foundation model that brings together electrocardiograms (ECG), heart sounds (PCG) and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR). It asks whether knowledge learned from cardiac imaging can improve screening with heart signals that are easier to record, with ECG linking the imaging and acoustic data. The wider aim is cardiovascular screening that works in resource-limited settings.

I work on the cardiac MRI and ECG–imaging data. That means preparing MRI datasets, selecting and standardising cardiac image sequences, assigning cardiac phases, building task labels, and organising the indices that pair ECGs with CMR scans for training and evaluation. The research plan also includes evaluating CineMA as an external baseline.

AI agents for business data

I'm helping build a natural-language business intelligence system that lets business users explore financial data by asking questions in plain English. It runs over Apache Fineract's 261-table PostgreSQL database and coordinates planning, SQL generation, read-only execution and explanation. Every answer comes with its evidence: the SQL, the tables and columns used, the business definitions and any assumptions.

I own the planner, the explanation layer and the user interface, including the workflow for capturing analyst corrections. To write answers, the language model produces a sentence template and application code fills in the returned values, so database results never go into model prompts. Analyst feedback becomes versioned business definitions, adopted only after a person approves them, so the system gets better at interpreting questions without changing the model. The aim throughout is AI-generated analysis that people can inspect and hold to account.

Software development and applied AI

My other work spans human detection in aerial search-and-rescue imagery, multi-agent AI systems and natural-language tools for exploring data. Before graduate school, I co-developed registration and staff appraisal applications at the Ghana-India Kofi Annan Centre of Excellence in ICT, using Python, FastAPI and PostgreSQL.

Across all of it, I care about careful implementation, reproducible experiments, and being clear about what a system can and cannot do.

Detection and segmentation, head to head

I compared YOLOv8, Faster R-CNN, EfficientDet and RetinaNet for detection, and SAM, CLIPSeg, U-Net, Mask R-CNN, DeepLabV3 and SegFormer for segmentation, on a dataset of bag images our group annotated. I traced and fixed a RetinaNet run that was returning zero predictions. The surprise: a U-Net trained from scratch beat the pretrained models, and classic Watershed beat them all.
